## 1. INTRODUCTION

350Solutions was contracted to perform an audit and validation of the production facility as well as verification of carbon dioxide removal credit (CORC) claims for Clean Maine Carbon's (CMC) biochar production and utilization. CMC utilizes a slow pyrolysis process at its Greenville, ME facility to produce biochar from woody biomass from regional forest waste.

Regional forestry management and lumber production operations provide sustainable process feedstock streams to support CMC operations. CMC's use of these residual by-product streams to produce biochar presents a higher value use that leads to carbon sequestration. CMC currently supplies biochar as a soil amendment application to several end users.

350Solutions conducted an audit of the process, lifecycle CO₂ emissions assessment (LCA), and other administrative details to verify compliance with the requirements of the Puro.Earth Standard General Rules V3.1 [1] and Biochar Methodology (Edition 2022 Version 3) [2]. The audit and verification included a document review, a detailed data audit, and a site visit on September 27th, 2024.

# 2. TECHNOLOGY DESCRIPTION

CMC operates a biochar production technology developed by BioMass Energy Techniques, Inc (BET), an original equipment manufacturer that owns the intellectual property for the BET BioMass Energy Systems. CMC utilizes a model BET 24PRD (pyrolysis rotary kiln). The rotary kiln is a pyro-processing device used to raise materials to a high temperature in a continuous process. Rotary kiln systems work by processing material in a rotating drum at high temperatures for a specified retention time to cause a physical change or chemical reaction in the material being processed. An aerial view of the facility in Greenville ME is shown in Figure 1.

# FIGURE 1. AERIAL VIEW OF THE CMC GREENVILLE FACILITY

As shown in Figure 2 below, the biochar manufacturing process is comprised of a combustion chamber (A), a retention chamber (B), a rotary kiln (C), a thermal oxidation chamber (E), and a water cooler. The combustion chamber A is where wood fuel is fed for heating of the process and primary combustion takes place. Up to 2,200 pounds per hour (lb/hr) of biomass (at an assumed moisture content of 40%) is gravity fed into this chamber. Chamber B is a high-temperature retention chamber which allows condensable gases to achieve complete combustion. A negative draft pulls the gases off the feedstock in Chamber A and into Chamber B.

# FIGURE 2. CMC’S BIOCHAR PRODUCTION PROCESS SCHEMATIC

Chamber C is a refractory-lined rotary drum pyrolysis kiln, in which thermal energy from Chamber B makes contact via countercurrent flow with the biomass feedstock (input into the process at point 1 in the diagram). Gases generated during the pyrolysis process in Chamber C increase the thermal energy of the system above the thermal energy in Chamber B. Chamber D is a thermal oxidation chamber that provides space and residence time to facilitate combustion of gases generated in Chamber C. Chamber E is the exhaust stack. CMC anticipates possible future scenarios that involve using the exhaust gases for other purposes including facility heating or power generation.

During typical operations, up to 1,000 lb/hr of green biomass feedstock is fed into the rotary drum at Point 1. An auger conveyance system moves the feedstock materials into the Chamber C rotary drum. The rotating drum moves the material towards the high temperature retention Chamber B exposing it to extreme heat which serves to complete the pyrolysis process. The size and rotational speed of the Chamber C rotary drum determines the retention time and can be adjusted by the operator. The feedstock material reaches its desired state, following pyrolysis at Stage 3, and drops down into the discharge area in Stage 4. This process is fed into a water cooler and exits the system at Point 5. There is also an additional discharge point biochar for the biomass used to heat the drum, which is seen in Figure 3.

Currently, CMC acquires feedstock from one regional lumber company. The feedstock material is waste hardwood from lumber operations (primarily oak, ash, and maple), processed into chips of 2 inches or less at the lumber mill. This feedstock is transported to the CMC facility in Greenville by truck with typical payloads ranging from 10-30 tons per load from sawmill residuals. The lumber mill currently has no other use for this waste material. The actual quantities, loads and sources for all feedstock delivered to the facility are tracked via trip tickets.

Feedstock stockpiled at the site is managed and fed into the biochar process by a diesel fuel powered front-end loader. The actual quantity of fuel used by this equipment is metered at an on-site fuel tank and equipment filling station and recorded on a weekly basis. The biochar production process is typically operated 24 hours per day, 5 days per week.

# FIGURE 3. CMC’S BIOCHAR PRODUCTION PROCESS

During normal operations, any supplemental energy demands of the process required to meet proper operating temperatures are provided by biomass fuel that is a subset of the feedstock used for biochar production. For plant startup, the combustion chamber is charged with wood fuel and ignited using a propane torch. The quantity of propane used for each startup is recorded at the on-site propane storage tanks. The plant’s process equipment utilizes electricity supplied by the regional electric grid. Electricity consumption of the biochar production process is not currently metered independently. Instead, conservative estimates of power consumption are reported based on readings from the revenue-grade, utility-owned meter installed at the facility. Meter readings are recorded at the utility meter with and without biochar production operations. These data allow operators to calculate a conservatively based estimate of the power consumed by the biochar production equipment and supporting electrical loads (lighting and controls).

Biochar leaving the pyrolysis kiln is quenched using potable water supplied by the local utility. The quantity of water used is estimated on a weekly basis. Critical process parameters to assure consistent, high-quality biochar include biomass feed rate, process temperatures, and pyrolysis residence time. Each of the key parameters are continuously monitored to assure consistent operating conditions.

After quenching, biochar is conveyed to a packaging process where it is placed into super sacks for storage and shipping. Each super sack contains nominal one ton of biochar at approximately 15 to 30% moisture content to ensure safe storage. Sacks of biochar are loaded onto trucks for delivery to regional farms for use as an agricultural amendment. End users typically apply up to 3 tons of biochar per acre using the same tractor-towed equipment that they use for application of other fertilizers and land treatments.

CMC collects biochar samples on a weekly basis for proximate analyses provided by a qualified laboratory. To date, two biochar samples have been analyzed according to the thresholds defined in voluntary quality standards for biochar, namely the International Biochar Initiative (IBI) Certification Program, Test Category A Parameters. CMC also performs on-site biochar moisture determinations every 4 to 5 hours during production.

Plans are in place to address this finding.

# 4.2.2. Opportunities for Improvement

Recommendations for improving the quality of data and accuracy of the LCA and CORC claims in the future include:

- Prioritizing the recommended improvements to quantification of dry biochar produced specified in Section 4.2.1 above (scale calibration for biochar mass and balance calibration for moisture biochar determinations).
- Monitored water use: There is no monitoring of water consumption used for quenching biochar and cooling certain pyrolysis system components. Although this is an omission from the Puro methodology requirements, LCA emissions associated with water use are estimated (based on moisture content of biochar product) and actual use is minimal. However, this lack of data could lead to an underestimation of the environmental impact related to water use and implementing a measurement of water use is recommended.
- On site biochar moisture determinations: In addition to calibration of the balance used for wet and dry measurement of biochar sample mass, a moisture analyzer such as Ohaus MB32 or equivalent is recommended. These instruments, designed for analyses using ASTM D1762-84 standard test method for chemical analysis of wood charcoal would add reliability and credibility to the on-site moisture determinations.
- Energy use MRV improvements: Consumption of diesel fuel for materials handling, propane for pyrolysis startup and stabilization, and electricity use for operation of the pyrolysis system and supporting infrastructure (all components of Eproduction), are currently not directly measured. To improve LCA accuracy and prevent over reporting of emissions due to conservative energy use estimates, it is recommended that the use of each energy source be measured and recorded for each weekly operating period. Bulk storage gauges for diesel and propane currently in use at the site are sufficient to create these weekly records. A second power meter specific to the biochar process energy use may be required to isolate and record the power consumption. These weekly energy use records can then be normalized to production rates during a given reporting period (e.g., unit of energy consumed/dry tonne biochar produced).
- Oxidizer Exhaust Emissions: A full regulatory emissions test of the system exhaust gas was conducted in October 2023. Emissions of all required pollutants were documented as well below the respective limits and there, it is not anticipated that the Maine DEP will require another test in the foreseeable future. As an indication of continued regulatory compliance, it is recommended that the facility continuously monitor and record oxidizer and exhaust stack temperature at a minimum, to demonstrate that operating conditions are consistent with those recorded during the compliance testing.
